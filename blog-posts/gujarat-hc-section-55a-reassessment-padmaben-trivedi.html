A Valuation Reference the AO Was Never Allowed to Make:
Gujarat HC Quashes Reassessment Over the Pre-2012 Section 55A Rule

When an Assessing Officer reopens a capital gains assessment on the strength of a District Valuation Officer's report that the law of the day did not permit him to obtain, the entire reassessment collapses. The Gujarat High Court explains why — and why the 2012 amendment cannot be pressed into service to save it.

A Division Bench of the Gujarat High Court, comprising Justice A.S. Supehia and Justice Vaibhavi D. Nanavati, has quashed reassessment proceedings initiated against the late Padmaben Zinabhai Trivedi for Assessment Year 2010-11. The court set aside the notice issued under Section 148 of the Income Tax Act, 1961, holding that the reopening rested entirely on a valuation reference that the Assessing Officer had no power to make under the law as it stood at the relevant time.

The judgment is a clean and useful restatement of a point that continues to trip up the Revenue in capital gains cases: the version of Section 55A that applied before 1 July 2012 allowed a reference to the District Valuation Officer (DVO) only where the value declared by the assessee was less than the fair market value. Where the assessee's declared value was higher, the AO simply had no jurisdiction to send the matter to the DVO — and any reassessment built on such a reference is without legal foundation.

Background: The Sale and the Cost of Acquisition

The assessee sold a parcel of land at Bhimpore, Nani Daman, on 24 June 2009 for a total consideration of Rs. 92,65,680. Because the land was a long-term capital asset, the computation of capital gains turned on its cost of acquisition — and, the land having been acquired before 1 April 1981, on its fair market value as on 1 April 1981, which the law permits an assessee to substitute for actual cost under Section 55(2).

The assessee adopted a fair market value as on 1 April 1981 of Rs. 81 per square metre, supported by a report from a registered valuer. Applying indexation to that base and deducting it from the sale consideration, she computed and offered long-term capital gains of Rs. 22,94,720 in her return for AY 2010-11.

The Reopening: A DVO Report and a Much Lower 1981 Value

Years later, the Revenue reopened the assessment. The Assessing Officer referred the valuation to the District Valuation Officer, who valued the land as on 1 April 1981 at a figure equivalent to roughly Rs. 1 per square metre — dramatically lower than the Rs. 81 per square metre the assessee had adopted.

A lower 1981 base value means a lower indexed cost of acquisition, which in turn means a higher taxable capital gain. On the strength of the DVO's figure, the AO formed the view that long-term capital gains of approximately Rs. 69,62,580 had escaped assessment, and issued a notice under Section 148 to reopen AY 2010-11.

Two features of that notice matter. First, it was issued on 30 March 2017 — the very last day of the six-year limitation window for the assessment year in question. Second, and more starkly, the assessee, Padmaben Zinabhai Trivedi, had already died on 21 May 2012 — nearly five years before the notice was issued — yet the Section 148 notice was issued in the name of the deceased.

The Core Legal Question: Did the AO Have the Power to Refer to the DVO?

Everything in this case flows from a single provision — Section 55A of the Income Tax Act, which governs when an Assessing Officer may refer the question of the fair market value of a capital asset to a Valuation Officer.

As it stood before 1 July 2012, clause (a) of Section 55A permitted a reference to the Valuation Officer only where, in the opinion of the AO, the value of the asset as claimed by the assessee was less than its fair market value. In other words, the machinery existed to catch assessees who had under-stated value — not those who had, in the Revenue's view, over-stated it.

The Finance Act, 2012 amended clause (a) with effect from 1 July 2012, replacing the words "is less than its fair market value" with "is at variance with its fair market value." That single change transformed the provision: after 1 July 2012, a reference could be made whenever the declared value was at variance with the fair market value — whether higher or lower.

Pre-2012 Section 55A(a): reference allowed only where declared value "is less than" fair market value.

Post-2012 Section 55A(a): reference allowed where declared value "is at variance with" fair market value.

The transaction in this case took place in June 2009, and the assessment year was 2010-11. The law that applied was the un-amended Section 55A. And under that law, because the assessee's declared value (Rs. 81 per sq. m.) was higher than what the Revenue believed to be the fair market value (about Rs. 1 per sq. m.), the AO's declared value was not less than the fair market value. The pre-condition for a DVO reference simply did not exist.

The Court's Reasoning: No Reference, No Reassessment

The Gujarat High Court held that the reference to the DVO was without jurisdiction. Since the assessee's declared fair market value exceeded the value the Revenue sought to adopt, the un-amended Section 55A(a) did not authorise the AO to refer the matter to the Valuation Officer at all.

That finding is fatal to the entire reassessment. The AO's belief that income had escaped assessment was founded exclusively on the DVO's report. Strip away the report — which the AO was never entitled to obtain — and there is nothing left to support the formation of the requisite "reason to believe." A reassessment cannot stand on material the AO had no power to gather.

The court also declined to allow the Finance Act, 2012 amendment to rescue the reopening. The amendment substituting "at variance with" for "is less than" is prospective, operating from 1 July 2012. It cannot be applied to a transaction of June 2009 or an assessment year of 2010-11 to retrospectively clothe the AO with a power he did not possess when the relevant events occurred.

The Precedents: Puja Prints and Hiaben Jayantilal Shah

The court's conclusion was not written on a blank slate. It rests on a well-settled line of authority.

In Commissioner of Income Tax v. Puja Prints (360 ITR 697), the Bombay High Court held squarely that under the pre-amendment Section 55A(a), a reference to the DVO could be made only where the value declared by the assessee was less than the fair market value — and that where the declared value was higher, the reference was impermissible. The 2012 amendment, the Bombay High Court held, was prospective and could not apply to earlier years.

The Gujarat High Court's own precedent in Hiaben Jayantilal Shah v. ITO (310 ITR 31) is to the same effect, confirming the limited scope of the pre-2012 provision. Read together, these decisions establish a consistent judicial position that the Revenue has repeatedly failed to overcome: a DVO reference under the old Section 55A(a) in an over-valuation scenario is a nullity.

The Dead-Person Notice: A Ground Left Open

There was a second, independent infirmity in the notice — it was issued in the name of a person who had died almost five years earlier. A notice under Section 148 issued against a dead person is, on a well-developed line of authority, a nullity, and the defect is not a curable procedural irregularity.

Gujarat itself has strong precedent on the point — including Chandreshbhai Jayantibhai Patel v. ITO and Rasid Lala / Urmilaben-type rulings — holding that reassessment notices issued in the name of the deceased, without valid substitution of the legal representative within limitation, cannot be sustained.

Because the court quashed the notice on the Section 55A jurisdictional ground, it did not find it necessary to finally adjudicate the dead-person challenge and left that question open. Practitioners should note the point but not over-read it: the ratio of this judgment is the valuation-jurisdiction point, and the dead-person issue remains available as a separate ground in an appropriate case.

Why This Judgment Matters

This decision is significant beyond its immediate facts, for several reasons.

1. The Pre-2012 Section 55A Bar Is Alive and Being Enforced

Old-year capital gains reassessments — particularly those turning on 1 April 1981 valuations of land acquired long ago — continue to surface. Whenever such a reopening rests on a DVO report obtained under the un-amended Section 55A in an over-valuation scenario, the reference is void and the reassessment collapses with it. This judgment is a fresh, citable reaffirmation of that rule from the Gujarat High Court.

2. A Void Reference Poisons the Entire Reassessment

The case is a clean illustration of a broader principle: where the sole foundation of the AO's "reason to believe" is material the AO had no lawful power to collect, the belief itself is unsustainable. It is not enough for the Revenue to point to a large apparent escapement — the route by which that escapement was discovered must itself be lawful.

3. Amendments Expanding Revenue Powers Are Read Prospectively

The court's refusal to apply the 2012 amendment backwards is consistent with the settled approach that provisions enlarging the Revenue's powers or an assessee's exposure are construed prospectively unless the statute clearly says otherwise. The date of the transaction and the assessment year — not the date of the reopening — fix the applicable law.

4. The Dead-Person Defence Remains a Distinct, Powerful Ground

Even though it was left open here, the dead-person point is a reminder that limitation-eve notices issued mechanically against assessees who are no longer alive remain highly vulnerable. Legal representatives should preserve and assert this ground independently rather than relying on it being subsumed within other challenges.

Practical Takeaways for Assessees and Practitioners

  • Check the vintage of the transaction first. If the capital gains relate to an assessment year before AY 2013-14 (transaction before 1 July 2012), the un-amended Section 55A applies. A DVO reference is then permissible only where the assessee's declared value is less than fair market value.
  • Identify over-valuation scenarios early. Where the AO seeks to reduce the assessee's declared 1981 value (thereby increasing the gain), and the year is pre-2012, the DVO reference is without jurisdiction — attack it at the threshold.
  • Cite Puja Prints and Hiaben Jayantilal Shah. These decisions squarely hold that the pre-amendment Section 55A(a) does not permit a reference in over-valuation cases, and that the 2012 amendment is prospective.
  • Attack the foundation, not just the quantum. If the DVO report is the sole basis for the reopening and the reference was void, the reassessment falls entirely — there is no need to litigate the correctness of the valuation itself.
  • Keep the registered valuer's report robust. A well-supported registered valuer's report for the 1 April 1981 value remains the assessee's first line of defence on the merits, quite apart from the jurisdictional argument.
  • Watch for dead-person and limitation-eve notices. Where the assessee has died before the notice, or the notice is issued on the last day of limitation without valid substitution of the legal representative, preserve and raise that defence as a separate ground.
  • Writ under Article 226 is a live option. Where the reopening is founded on a jurisdictional defect apparent on the face of the record — such as a void Section 55A reference — approaching the High Court directly, rather than exhausting the assessment process, is both available and advisable.
