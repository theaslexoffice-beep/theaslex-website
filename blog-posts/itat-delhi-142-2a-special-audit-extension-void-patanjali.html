The Wrong Officer Signed the Extension:
ITAT Delhi Voids a Rs. 208 Crore Assessment in Patanjali Ayurved

A special audit under Section 142(2A) needs the Commissioner's approval. An extension of time to complete that audit does not — it is the Assessing Officer's call, and his alone. When the Principal Commissioner granted the extension instead, the Delhi Tribunal held the entire assessment void ab initio. A study in why, in tax administration, the identity of the signatory is not a formality.

A Division Bench of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, Delhi Bench 'H', comprising Shri Kul Bharat, Judicial Member and Dr. B. R. R. Kumar, Accountant Member, has dismissed the Revenue's appeal in DCIT, Circle-9(1), New Delhi v. Patanjali Ayurved Ltd. and upheld the quashing of an assessment framed on a total income of Rs. 208,31,63,890 for Assessment Year 2014-15.

The assessment did not fall on the merits of a single addition. It fell because of one letter, signed by one officer who was not entitled to sign it. The extension of time for completing the special audit under Section 142(2A) was granted by the Principal Commissioner. Under the proviso to Section 142(2C), that power is vested in the Assessing Officer and in no one else. The Tribunal held that the substitution was fatal, and that the assessment which followed was void ab initio.

For anyone who litigates on either side of an ITAT bench, this is a judgment worth reading closely — not because the proposition is new, but because it is now backed by an affirmation from the jurisdictional High Court and has begun to convert a technical objection into a routine, and frequently decisive, threshold challenge.

Background: A Revised Return, a Doubt, and a Special Audit

Patanjali Ayurved Ltd. is engaged in the manufacture and trading of fast-moving consumer goods. For AY 2014-15 it filed its return on 28 November 2014 declaring a total income of Rs. 83,53,21,350. A revised return followed on 27 November 2015, declaring Rs. 138,49,25,610.

The Assessing Officer, entertaining doubts about the correctness of the accounts, issued a show cause notice on 27 November 2017 asking why a special audit under Section 142(2A) should not be directed, having regard to the nature and complexity of the accounts. After considering the assessee's reply and after obtaining the approval of the Commissioner of Income Tax by letter dated 22 December 2017, the AO directed the special audit by letter dated 26 December 2017.

The special audit report was eventually furnished on 2 July 2018 under Section 142(2C). The AO then completed the assessment under Section 143(3) read with Section 144C on 24 August 2018, assessing income at Rs. 208,31,63,890 — an addition of roughly Rs. 69.82 crore over the revised return.

In between the direction and the report lies the letter that decided this appeal. By letter dated 27 March 2018, the AO informed the assessee:

"In this regard, it is submitted and intimated that the Ld. Pr. CIT-7, New Delhi has accorded approval for extension of period by 90 days for special audit u/s 142(2A) in the above mentioned case for AY 2014-15."

The AO, in other words, did not extend the period. He conveyed somebody else's extension.

The Statutory Architecture: Two Different Officers, Two Different Powers

The case turns on a distinction that is easy to miss and impossible to argue around once it is seen. Sections 142(2A) and 142(2C) allocate two separate powers to two separate authorities.

Section 142(2A) — the direction: AO acts, Commissioner approves

Section 142(2A) permits the Assessing Officer, having regard to the nature and complexity of the accounts, the volume of the accounts, doubts about their correctness, the multiplicity of transactions or the specialised nature of the business, and the interests of the revenue, to direct the assessee to get the accounts audited by a nominated accountant. He may do so only "with the previous approval of the Principal Chief Commissioner or Chief Commissioner or Principal Commissioner or Commissioner." A proviso requires that the assessee first be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard.

Section 142(2C) — the extension: AO alone

Section 142(2C) requires the report to be furnished within such period as the Assessing Officer may specify. Its proviso then reads:

"Provided that the Assessing Officer may, suo motu, or on an application made in this behalf by the assessee and for any good and sufficient reason, extend the said period by such further period or periods as he thinks fit; so, however, that the aggregate of the period originally fixed and the period or periods so extended shall not, in any case, exceed one hundred and eighty days from the date on which the direction under sub-section (2A) is received by the assessee."

There is no reference to the Commissioner in the proviso. Not by way of approval, not by way of concurrence, not at all. Where Parliament wanted a superior officer in the loop it said so expressly, in sub-section (2A). Its silence in sub-section (2C) is not an oversight to be filled by administrative practice.

The Tribunal in Soul Space put the logic of the design plainly: the initial direction for a special audit is a serious step, so the legislature required the subject matter to be examined by a higher and more experienced officer before it is taken. Once that scrutiny has happened at the threshold, there is no reason for the higher authority to be involved again for a mere extension — Parliament left that decision with the officer conducting the assessment, on conditions it specified.

Why an Invalid Extension Destroys the Assessment

A reader encountering the point for the first time may reasonably ask why the identity of the officer granting an extension should annihilate an assessment. The answer lies in limitation.

A special audit under Section 142(2A) stops the clock. The period from the date on which the AO's direction is received by the assessee up to the last date on which the assessee is required to furnish the report is excluded in computing the limitation period for completing the assessment. That exclusion is the reason special audits are so often directed in the closing weeks of a limitation window — a point the Commissioner (Appeals) made expressly below, describing the direction as having been passed just before the time-barring date and functioning, in substance, as a tool for extension of time.

If the extension of the audit period is itself without authority, the extended period cannot be excluded. The clock never stopped for those additional ninety days. The assessment that was completed in reliance on that exclusion was therefore completed beyond limitation — and an assessment framed after limitation has expired is not a defective order capable of correction. It is a nullity.

That is precisely how the Tribunal in Soul Space expressed the consequence: the extension being beyond the powers vested by the statute, "the assessment completed after the due date is held to be void ab initio."

The Tribunal's Reasoning in Patanjali

The core question framed by the Commissioner (Appeals) was narrow: whether the Principal Commissioner had exceeded his jurisdiction in granting approval for the extension of the time limit for conducting the special audit.

The Commissioner (Appeals) answered it in the affirmative, and the Tribunal affirmed. The reasoning proceeds in three moves.

1. A statutory discretion exercised on another's direction is no discretion at all

The Commissioner (Appeals) invoked the Supreme Court's decision in Anirudhsinhji Karansinhji Jadeja v. State of Gujarat [(1995) 5 SCC 302], which holds that where a statutory authority is vested with jurisdiction, it must exercise that jurisdiction according to its own discretion; if the discretion is exercised under the direction of, or in compliance with the instructions of, a higher authority, that is a failure to exercise discretion altogether.

2. Satisfaction cannot be borrowed, and powers cannot be interchanged

The jurisdictional Delhi High Court's decision in CIT v. SPL's Siddhartha Ltd. [(2012) 345 ITR 223 (Del) / 17 taxmann.com 138] was applied. The passage extracted by the Tribunal is worth quoting for its breadth:

"Section 116 of the Act also defines the Income Tax Authorities as different and distinct Authorities... If powers conferred on a particular authority are arrogated by other authority without mandate of law, it will create chaos in the administration of law and hierarchy of administration will mean nothing. Satisfaction of one authority cannot be substituted by the satisfaction of the other authority. It is trite that when a statute requires a thing to be done in a certain manner, it shall be done in that manner alone..."

The High Court went on to hold that where a particular authority has been designated to record satisfaction on an issue, it is that authority alone who must apply an independent mind, and the satisfaction recorded must be "independent" and not "borrowed" or "dictated." The Patna High Court's formulation in Sheo Narain Jaiswal v. ITO [176 ITR 35 (Pat.)] was cited to the same effect: where an AO does not himself exercise jurisdiction but merely acts at the behest of a superior authority, the assumption of jurisdiction is bad for non-satisfaction of the condition precedent.

3. Seniority does not cure incompetence of authority

The most quotable line in the chain comes from Soul Space, adopted wholesale by the Commissioner (Appeals) and by the Tribunal here:

"A power which has been given to a specified authority has to be discharged only by him. Substitution of that officer/authority by any other officer, may be of higher rank, cannot validate the said order/action."

This is the answer to the intuitive but wrong objection that an extension blessed by a Principal Commissioner is a fortiori better than one signed by an Assessing Officer. Competence under a taxing statute is not a hierarchy of quality; it is an allocation. An officer senior to the one Parliament named is, for that purpose, simply a different officer.

The Tribunal also examined whether the AO's letter of 27 March 2018 could be read as the AO himself extending the period, with the reference to the Principal Commissioner being mere administrative intimation. It could not. On the language of the letter, the AO conveyed an approval; he did not exercise a power. There was no independent application of mind by the AO to the existence of "good and sufficient reason" under the proviso, which is the very thing the proviso requires him to consider.

The Precedent Chain — and Why It Is Now Materially Stronger

The Commissioner (Appeals) had relied on the Tribunal's decision in ACIT v. Soul Space Projects Ltd. [ITA No. 193/Del/2015, order dated 3 June 2020], where an extension had similarly been granted by the CIT (Central)-II and merely conveyed by the AO.

The Tribunal in Patanjali added a significant fact that post-dated the appellate order below. The Soul Space decision has since been affirmed by the jurisdictional Delhi High Court in Pr. Commissioner of Income Tax (Central) v. Soul Space Projects Ltd., by order dated 11 December 2023 — while the Commissioner (Appeals)'s order in Patanjali was dated 11 August 2023 and had relied on the Tribunal order alone.

That sequence matters. A proposition that rested on a Tribunal decision when the first appellate authority applied it had, by the time the Tribunal heard the Revenue's appeal, hardened into jurisdictional High Court authority. The Revenue's appeal was, in effect, overtaken by its own losses elsewhere.

There is also internal consistency within the assessee's own record. The Commissioner (Appeals) noted that in Patanjali's own case for AY 2012-13, by order dated 21 September 2020 in Appeal No. CIT(A), Delhi-7/10162/2019-20, the assessment had been held void ab initio on identical facts for the same failure to follow the mandatory procedure under Sections 142(2A) and 142(2C).

The Revenue's Third Ground: "You Decided Only the Technical Issue"

The Revenue's third ground of appeal deserves separate attention, because it is the argument every Departmental Representative is tempted to make when a large assessment is lost on a threshold point: that the Commissioner (Appeals) erred in "considering only the technical issue leaving other substantial issues on which addition has been made."

The argument has emotional force and no legal traction. Where an assessment order is void ab initio, there is no order on which the merits can operate. An appellate authority does not adjudicate additions made by an order that, in the eye of law, was never validly made. The "substantial issues" are not being ignored; there is simply nothing left to attach them to.

The Tribunal did not labour the point. Having found that the procedure adopted for the special audit "suffers from multiple infirmities," it held the AO's order void ab initio, affirmed the Commissioner (Appeals), dismissed the Revenue's appeal, and dismissed the assessee's cross objection as infructuous.

Why This Judgment Matters

1. The special audit file is now a standard place to look for a jurisdictional defect

Special audits are directed in the biggest and most document-heavy assessments, and almost always against a limitation deadline. That combination — high stakes, time pressure, multi-officer processing — is exactly the environment in which procedural shortcuts get taken. Every 142(2A) case should now be examined not for what the auditor found, but for who signed what, and when.

2. Approval architecture is becoming the most productive line of attack in tax litigation

The reasoning here is not confined to Section 142(2C). It is the same reasoning that has invalidated reassessments for approvals taken from the wrong authority under Section 151, search assessments for defective satisfaction under Section 153C, and penalties levied without the prior approval mandated by Section 274(2) — an example the Tribunal itself invoked. Where a statute names an officer, that naming is a jurisdictional fact.

3. The 180-day outer limit remains an independent trap

Even a validly granted extension cannot take the aggregate period beyond 180 days from the date the Section 142(2A) direction is received by the assessee. This ceiling is absolute and no authority, however senior, can relax it. It should be computed independently in every case, even where the extension was signed by the right officer.

4. The Income-tax Act, 2025 does not make the point academic

For years falling under the new statute, the corresponding special audit provisions must be mapped and their allocation of powers read afresh — the question in every case being which authority the section names for the direction and which it names for any extension. But the underlying principle is not a creature of the 1961 Act at all. That a power allocated to a named authority cannot be exercised by another is a principle of administrative law, and it survives re-codification.

Practical Takeaways — For the Assessee

  • Requisition the entire special audit correspondence at the outset. Not just the direction and the report — the show cause notice, the reply, the approval letter from the Commissioner, every extension letter, and the internal communications between the AO, the Addl. CIT and the Pr. CIT. The defect in this case was visible only in the covering letters.
  • Read the extension letter for who exercised the power, not who signed the envelope. Language such as "the Pr. CIT has accorded approval for extension" is the tell. An AO exercising his own power under the proviso to Section 142(2C) records his own satisfaction of "good and sufficient reason"; he does not relay someone else's approval.
  • Check whose application triggered the extension. The proviso permits the AO to act suo motu or on an application by the assessee. An extension granted on the special auditor's request, without either of those foundations, is an additional independent infirmity.
  • Compute the 180-day ceiling and the limitation date on paper, twice. Strip out any invalid extension and re-run the Section 153 computation. If the assessment falls outside the period, the ground is complete and does not depend on the merits.
  • Take the point at the earliest opportunity and keep it alive. Raise it before the AO, press it before the first appellate authority, and preserve it in a cross objection. Note that in this case the assessee's own cross objection on limitation was not pressed and was ultimately dismissed as infructuous — a reminder that the surviving ground was the one argued fully below.
  • Cite the affirmation, not just the Tribunal order. The persuasive weight now sits with the Delhi High Court's order dated 11 December 2023 in Pr. CIT (Central) v. Soul Space Projects Ltd., with SPL's Siddhartha Ltd. behind it.

Practical Takeaways — For the Revenue

  • The extension order must be an order of the Assessing Officer. If administrative practice requires the range head to be informed, inform him — but the operative document must record the AO's own satisfaction of good and sufficient reason and his own decision to extend, in his own words.
  • Do not treat the Section 142(2A) approval as travelling downstream. The Commissioner's approval attaches to the direction. It does not carry into the extension, and arguing that his involvement is "inherent" has already been rejected in Soul Space.
  • A merits case cannot rescue a void order. Where the threshold point is lost, the ground that the first appellate authority "decided only the technical issue" is not an answer. The better course is to identify the defect before the assessment is framed, while there may still be time to cure it.
  • Audit the timing narrative. A special audit directed on the eve of the limitation date invites the inference — drawn expressly by the Commissioner (Appeals) here — that it was a device to buy time. Contemporaneous material showing genuine complexity of accounts is the only durable answer to that inference.
